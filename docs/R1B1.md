R1-B1 should be implemented as a **multilayer physical model that starts from the microscopic degrees of freedom, solves whether a stable or quasi-stable state of matter exists after two-dimensionalization, and derives the current response from that state**, rather than first building a two-dimensional Drude model and then searching for parameters that let it pass the f-sum and Kramers–Kronig tests.
 
 
I suggest dividing R1-B1 into two interconnected core problems that cannot substitute for each other:
 
 
1. **Matter question:** In a three-dimensional material under DCC geometric evolution, how do the original particles, bound states, and interactions change? Can a new low-dimensional stable state form?
 
 
2. **Response question:** If such a matter state exists, what are its bands, carriers, scattering, and current response? Can one obtain a \\(\sigma_s(\omega)\\) with finite spectral weight and causal self-consistency?
 
 
If the first problem has no physical solution, the second has no legitimate material input. This also means R1-B1 cannot take \\(n\_{2D},g,v_F,\omega\_{\rm end}\\) as four fitting inputs.
 
 
# **I. First, determine what R1-B1 physically is**
 
 
## **1.1 Do not presuppose what the matter is after two-dimensionalization**
 
 
At least four classes of outcomes must be allowed to emerge naturally from the model:
 
 
| Branch | Physical meaning          | R1-B1 questions to answer           |
 
 
| -- | ------------- | ---------------------- |
 
 
| A  | partial or complete preservation of the original material structure | can atomic, nuclear, and electronic structures remain stable under the new geometry?    |
 
 
| B  | original structure fails, forming a new matter state | do new bound states, collective states, or low-dimensional condensed states exist?   |
 
 
| C  | no stable final state         | do dissociation, particle escape, or continuous energy dissipation occur?    |
 
 
| D  | other non-equilibrium or quasi-stable states   | are there states with finite lifetime, non-equilibrium character, or spatial inhomogeneity? |
 
 
Here we follow the distinction you already made between R1-A and R1-B:
 
 
- R1-A studies when the original structure fails;
 
 
- R1-B studies whether a new state can form after failure.
 
 
**The B branch must not be taken as the default target.** If the solution is C or D, that result must also be reported faithfully.
 
 
## **1.2 Fix the geometric input**
 
 
Under the current DCC geometric convention, the z direction has the intrinsic length:
 
 
\\[ d\ell_z=B(t)\\,dz. \\]
 
 
Therefore, if the system under study is compressed along the z direction, one cannot simply reduce a thickness parameter; instead, the microscopic dynamics must genuinely feel the time-dependent geometry.
 
 
The simplest spatial metric is schematically:
 
 
\\[ ds^2=-c^2dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+B^2(t)dz^2. \\]
 
 
Here \\(B(t)\\) is a geometric input, not a quantity freely fitted by the material model.
 
 
The following must also be specified:
 
 
- \\(B(t)\\) evolution path;
 
 
- compression rate and its timescale;
 
 
- transverse boundary conditions;
 
 
- initial particle composition and total particle number;
 
 
- inputs of energy, momentum, and external constraints;
 
 
- whether the geometric change does work on the matter, and how energy is exchanged.
 
 
This step is especially important: **the same final thickness does not guarantee the same final matter state.** Rapid compression and quasi-static compression may follow different non-equilibrium paths.
 
 
# **II. Establish the microscopic Hamiltonian, rather than establishing the conductivity formula first**
 
 
This is the most central task of R1-B1 by a wide margin.
 
 
## **2.1 First establish an inventory of degrees of freedom**
 
 
Do not assume from the outset that there is only a two-dimensional electron gas. At least the following degrees of freedom must be checked:
 
 
| Degrees of freedom   | Physics to be described            |
 
 
| ----- | ------------------ |
 
 
| Electrons    | electronic energy spectrum, occupation, trapping, escape, and interactions |
 
 
| Atomic nuclei   | nuclear structure, internuclear interactions, and possible nuclear reactions    |
 
 
| Electromagnetic field   | charge interactions, screening, and collective electromagnetic response   |
 
 
| Bound states   | atoms, molecules, ions, or novel low-dimensional bound states   |
 
 
| Collective degrees of freedom | density waves, condensed states, polarization, and other collective excitations  |
 
 
| Geometric degrees of freedom | DCC scale changes and material stress coupling   |
 
 
Not all degrees of freedom necessarily need to be retained explicitly in the final calculation, but there must be a justified basis for deciding which can be integrated out and which can be treated effectively.
 
 
In particular, after electronic bound states have already disappeared, one must not continue to use orbitals, cross sections, and material parameters from ordinary atomic or molecular models.
 
 
## **2.2 Establish a microscopic action that varies with geometry**
 
 
Formally, one can start from:
 
 
\\[ S=S\_{\rm matter}+S\_{\rm EM}+S\_{\rm interaction} \\]
 
 
Start.
 
 
For example:
 
 
\\[ S\_{\rm matter} = \int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\\, \mathcal L\_{\rm matter} (\psi,g\_{\mu
 
 
u}), \\]
 
 
\\[ S\_{\rm EM} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} \int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\\, F\_{\mu
 
 
u}F^{\mu
 
 
u}. \\]
 
 
Here \\(\psi\\) represents the actually selected matter degrees of freedom. The interaction term must be constructed from the chosen physical theory; the effective parameters of ordinary three-dimensional materials cannot simply be carried over unchanged to the extremely compressed system.
 
 
The action then yields:
 
 
- equations of motion for matter;
 
 
- electromagnetic field equations;
 
 
- current operator;
 
 
- energy-momentum tensor;
 
 
- particle-number and charge conservation relations.
 
 
If \\(B(t)\\) is an externally prescribed time-dependent background, the energy input into and output from the matter due to the geometric background must also be handled correctly.
 
 
## **2.3 An unavoidable scale problem**
 
 
The target thickness you previously recorded is:
 
 
\\[ d\_{\parallel}=6.7420\ {\rm fm}. \\]
 
 
This differs enormously from ordinary atomic scales. According to earlier records, ordinary bound states cease to be directly applicable at roughly \\(\pi a_0=1.66\\) Å and below.
 
 
This means one cannot use:
 
 
> ordinary atomic structure → ordinary molecular structure → ordinary two-dimensional material → adjust the thickness parameter
 
 
such a simple extrapolation route.
 
 
If the final thickness really reaches fm scale, the model must re-examine the applicability of electrons, atomic nuclei, interactions, and relativistic effects. Conventional condensed-matter DFT, effective-mass models, or ordinary two-dimensional electron-gas models may not serve as the foundational theory without derivation.
 
 
**This is likely the hardest part of R1-B1 by far: one must first determine exactly which physical degrees of freedom still exist at that scale.**
 
 
# **III. Solve for the matter state: first answer whether a solution exists, then discuss material parameters**
 
 
Once the microscopic Hamiltonian has been established, the next stage is not to compute optical properties immediately but to solve for the matter state.
 
 
## **3.1 Particle composition and conservation constraints**
 
 
At least the following must be solved simultaneously:
 
 
\\[ N_i=\text{the numbers of each particle species or the corresponding conserved quantities} \\]
 
 
and:
 
 
\\[ Q\_{\rm total}=\text{total charge}, \\]
 
 
and determine, based on the actual physical processes, which particle numbers are strictly conserved and which may change through reactions, trapping, or escape.
 
 
The N7 gate you previously set out must be implemented explicitly here:
 
 
**Conserving the total electron number alone does not mean the carrier state has been determined.**
 
 
It is also necessary to distinguish:
 
 
- free carriers;
 
 
- bound electrons;
 
 
- trapped electrons;
 
 
- other charged particles;
 
 
- particle species that may participate in reactions.
 
 
If different matter components can convert into one another, the corresponding reaction channels and chemical equilibrium or non-equilibrium dynamics must be introduced.
 
 
This step should output the full particle composition, not just a single \\(n\_{2D}\\).
 
 
## **3.2 Solve for the energy and equation of state**
 
 
For a given geometric state and particle composition, solve:
 
 
\\[ E=E(n_i,B,\ldots) \\]
 
 
and compute the corresponding stress from the same energy functional or action.
 
 
The following must be checked:
 
 
1. whether the energy has a lower bound;
 
 
2. whether an equilibrium solution exists;
 
 
3. whether the equilibrium solution is stable against allowed perturbations;
 
 
4. whether dissociation, escape, or phase-transition channels exist;
 
 
5. whether metastable states with finite lifetime exist;
 
 
6. whether the state is stable against transverse-size and local-density perturbations.
 
 
The system cannot be declared stable simply by assigning an arbitrary pressure or stress value. Stress must be computed forward from the microscopic state and interactions.
 
 
## **3.3 Distinguish static stability from dynamic reachability**
 
 
Even if a stable solution exists at the final geometry, that does not mean the three-dimensional matter can reach it along the given DCC evolution path.
 
 
Two types of calculation are therefore needed:
 
 
**Static problem**
 
 
\\[ \frac{\delta E}{\delta\Psi}=0 \\]
 
 
Search for candidate equilibrium states and check their stability.
 
 
**Dynamic problem**
 
 
\\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = H[B(t)]\Psi \\]
 
 
or relativistic field equations appropriate to the chosen theory, to study how the matter evolves during the actual compression.
 
 
Neither can substitute for the other.
 
 
For example, a certain low-dimensional state may exist at the final geometry, but during rapid compression the system may already have dissociated and be unable to enter that state.
 
 
## **3.4 Stability acceptance criteria**
 
 
I suggest setting at least the following independent acceptance items:
 
 
| Acceptance item       | What must be demonstrated                  |
 
 
| --------- | ----------------------- |
 
 
| S-STATE-1 | the candidate state satisfies all conservation constraints            |
 
 
| S-STATE-2 | energy is bounded and a physically acceptable solution exists          |
 
 
| S-STATE-3 | linear perturbations do not lead to unacceptable growing modes        |
 
 
| S-STATE-4 | no immediate dissociation or collapse under nonlinear perturbations         |
 
 
| S-STATE-5 | under the given DCC path, the state has dynamic reachability.     |
 
 
| S-STATE-6 | if it is a metastable state, its lifetime exceeds the defined observation or experimental timescale |
 
 
These are not six pass thresholds to be set in advance, but six classes of questions for which evidence must be provided separately.
 
 
# **IV. Derive the optical response from the solved matter state**
 
 
This stage is entered only after the actual matter state has been determined above.
 
 
This part corresponds directly to your current G6/DC2 blockage.
 
 
## **4.1 First compute the true energy spectrum**
 
 
We obtain from the microscopic Hamiltonian the following:
 
 
\\[ E_n(\mathbf k) \\]
 
 
or, in systems without translational symmetry, obtain the corresponding eigenstates, spectral functions, and density of states.
 
 
Only then can the following be determined:
 
 
\\[ n\_{2D},\quad g,\quad v_F,\quad \omega\_{\rm end}. \\]
 
 
Their definitions should be:
 
 
| Output                      | Correct source               |
 
 
| ----------------------- | ------------------ |
 
 
| \\(n\_{2D}\\)           | actual carrier occupation and areal-density integral      |
 
 
| \\(g\\)                 | actual degeneracy of bands or eigenstates       |
 
 
| \\(v_F\\)               | derivative of the actual band dispersion          |
 
 
| \\(\omega\_{\rm end}\\) | cutoff or band edge determined by the actual band and transition structure |
 
 
For example, if the system really has an approximately linear dispersion:
 
 
\\[ E(\mathbf k)\simeq\hbar v_F|\mathbf k|, \\]
 
 
then:
 
 
\\[ v_F=\frac{1}{\hbar} \left|
 
 
abla\_{\mathbf k}E(\mathbf k)\right|. \\]
 
 
If the energy spectrum is not linear, one cannot force a single \\(v_F\\) to describe the whole frequency range.
 
 
## **4.2 The current operator must be derived from the same Hamiltonian without exception**
 
 
One cannot independently specify a convenient optical conductivity coefficient.
 
 
With an appropriate electromagnetic coupling convention, the current operator should be obtained by variation with respect to the vector potential:
 
 
\\[ \hat J_i = -\frac{\delta H}{\delta A_i}. \\]
 
 
Then compute the current correlation function.
 
 
The core of linear response is the Kubo formula:
 
 
\\[ \sigma\_{ij}(\omega) = \frac{1}{i(\omega+i0^+)} \left[ \Pi\_{ij}^{R}(\omega,\mathbf q=0) + \text{the corresponding contact term} \right]. \\]
 
 
The specific signs and contact term must be consistent with the adopted Fourier transform, gauge, and current definition.
 
 
What matters is:
 
 
- Bands come from the same Hamiltonian;
 
 
- The current operator comes from the same Hamiltonian;
 
 
- interband matrix elements come from the same set of eigenstates;
 
 
- the intraband response also comes from the same correlation function;
 
 
- One must not stitch together f-sum formulas from different models.
 
 
## **4.3&#x20;**\\(f\_{\rm intra}\\)**&#x20;must be obtained from the response decomposition**
 
 
A suggested definition is:
 
 
\\[ W\_{\rm total} = W\_{\rm intra}+W\_{\rm inter}, \\]
 
 
\\[ f\_{\rm intra} = \frac{W\_{\rm intra}}{W\_{\rm total}}. \\]
 
 
Note, however, that in an interacting system the separation into intraband and interband parts may depend on band projection, frequency range, or the definition of the effective theory. The specification must therefore dictate a concrete decomposition scheme.
 
 
One workable procedure is:
 
 
1. Obtain the full \\(\sigma(\omega)\\) from the microscopic current response;
 
 
2. Identify intraband and interband transitions via eigenstate or band projections;
 
 
3. Compute the spectral weight of each part;
 
 
4. Check whether the sum of the two parts recovers the full f-sum;
 
 
5. Vary the computational basis, cutoffs, and numerical precision to check the stability of the decomposition.
 
 
If a unique separation is impossible, report the decomposition dependence or an uncertainty interval rather than forcing out a precise \\(f\_{\rm intra}\\).
 
 
**In particular, the current module&#x20;**\\(f\_{\rm intra}=1\\)**&#x20;must not continue to be used as an input.**
 
 
## **4.4 τ should not be specified in advance**
 
 
For a genuinely interacting system, the full current response should be computed first.
 
 
Relaxation information can be obtained from the following sources:
 
 
- microscopic scattering processes;
 
 
- self-energies and corresponding vertex corrections;
 
 
- poles or spectral peak widths of the current response function;
 
 
- memory functions or transport kernels under appropriate conditions.
 
 
If the final response can indeed be approximated as:
 
 
\\[ \sigma_D(\omega) = \frac{\sigma_0}{1-i\omega\tau}, \\]
 
 
Only then can one report an effective \\(\tau\\).
 
 
Moreover, one must specify what this \\(\tau\\) corresponds to:
 
 
- single-particle lifetime;
 
 
- transport relaxation time;
 
 
- the relaxation time of a particular current mode;
 
 
- or an effective fitting time over some frequency interval.
 
 
They must not be collectively called a single relaxation time without any physical origin.
 
 
If the response contains multiple relaxation modes, non-exponential decay, or significant frequency dependence, the full response should be retained rather than forced into a single-pole Drude model.
 
 
# **V. Embed the f-sum and KK constraints into the model from the very beginning**
 
 
This is not a final patch-up stage; it should be part of the microscopic response calculation.
 
 
## **5.1 f-sum must come from the same microscopic theory**
 
 
For applicable non-relativistic continuous systems, the usual sum rule has the form:
 
 
\\[ W\_{\rm total} = \frac{\pi n e^2}{2m} \\]
 
 
but in band systems, interacting systems, non-parabolic dispersions, or relativistic systems the specific expression may differ.
 
 
Therefore one cannot specify a universal formula in advance and then apply it to all matter states.
 
 
The correct order is:
 
 
\\[ H \longrightarrow \hat J \longrightarrow \Pi^R \longrightarrow \sigma(\omega) \longrightarrow W\_{\rm total}. \\]
 
 
If an effective low-energy model is used, its range of validity in energy must be stated, as must the way the integrated-out high-energy degrees of freedom contribute to the spectral weight.
 
 
In particular, an isolated, infinitely extended Dirac cone model must not be taken directly as the f-sum model of a complete material without handling high-energy completeness and regularization.
 
 
## **5.2 KK must be verified against the full response**
 
 
Compute:
 
 
\\[ \operatorname{Re}\sigma(\omega), \qquad \operatorname{Im}\sigma(\omega) \\]
 
 
After that, independently check whether the two satisfy the Kramers–Kronig relation.
 
 
One cannot specify a real part first and then attach an unrelated imaginary part.
 
 
If a finite bandwidth, finite band set, or effective cutoff is adopted, the causal partner must be recomputed consistently with that full response.
 
 
## **5.3 Parameter searches must not be used to manufacture passing results**
 
 
The following should be explicitly prohibited:
 
 
- Scan \\(\tau\\) to find a passing result;
 
 
- Adjust \\(A\_{\rm INTER}\\) to fill a spectral-weight shortfall;
 
 
- Changing the material band edge to pass S4 is not acceptable;
 
 
- Artificially suppressing the interband weight to pass S5 is not acceptable;
 
 
- Selecting a particular matter branch in order to obtain a better-looking reflectivity.
 
 
All response parameters must come from the already determined matter state; they cannot determine the matter state in reverse.
 
 
# **VI. Numerical implementation: do not choose a universal solver from the outset**
 
 
R1-B1 will likely require multi-scale, multi-method collaboration. Different physical degrees of freedom call for different numerical methods.
 
 
| Object of study     | Candidate methods                             | Main use           |
 
 
| -------- | -------------------------------- | -------------- |
 
 
| Electronic structure     | many-body electronic-structure methods and, where applicable, DFT              | bands, eigenstates, occupation      |
 
 
| Strongly correlated systems    | Exact Diagonalization, DMRG, QMC etc. | correlations, bound states, phase transitions      |
 
 
| Nuclei and extreme-density matter | corresponding nuclear many-body theories and relativistic field theories                   | nuclear structure, nuclear interactions, particle composition |
 
 
| Electromagnetic response     | Kubo numerical calculations, spectral-function methods                  | current response, optical conductivity      |
 
 
| Non-equilibrium evolution    | real-time many-body dynamics and suitable field-equation solvers                | state evolution during compression     |
 
 
| Stability      | linearized eigenvalue analysis and nonlinear dynamics                  | unstable modes, metastable-state lifetimes     |
 
 
No method can be asserted in advance to be definitely applicable here.
 
 
For example, conventional DFT may be useful for ordinary condensed-matter materials, but for fm scale geometric compression, its degrees of freedom and effective interactions must first be shown to remain applicable. The mere fact that DFT has mature software does not justify choosing it as the foundational theory.
 
 
I suggest adopting a layered architecture:
 
 
**Layer 0 — DCC geometry and initial conditions**
 
 
B(t), compression path, initial composition, conservation constraints
 
 
**Layer 1 — microscopic matter Hamiltonian**
 
 
degrees of freedom, interactions, electromagnetic coupling, particle-number conservation
 
 
**Layer 2 — matter state solution**
 
 
composition, energy, equation of state, bound states, stability
 
 
**Layer 3 — microscopic current response**
 
 
energy spectrum, current operator, Kubo, scattering, transition matrix elements
 
 
**Layer 4 — optical response acceptance**
 
 
f-sum, KK, spectral weight redistribution, Drude reducibility, σ_s(ω)
 
 
Each layer should have its own input contract, output contract, and failure states. An upper layer must not treat the failed result of a lower layer as a definite input.
 
 
# **VII. Suggested practical implementation order**
 
 
I do not recommend immediately writing a complete R1-B1 solver program. First produce a theoretical specification, then decide on computational methods. Concretely, this can be divided into six phases.
 
 
## **Phase 0: Establishing the R1-B1 microscopic model specification**
 
 
Can begin immediately
 
 
First deliver a standalone specification that states clearly:
 
 
1. what the initial matter is;
 
 
2. which microscopic degrees of freedom are adopted;
 
 
3. which particle numbers are conserved;
 
 
4. which interactions must be retained;
 
 
5. DCC geometry couples to Hamiltonian;
 
 
6. which state branches are allowed;
 
 
7. under what conditions the model is considered to have failed.
 
 
No numerical simulations are run at this stage.
 
 
**Acceptance criterion:** no unjustified assumptions about material parameters, degeneracies, effective masses, or carrier composition.
 
 
## **Phase 1: Establishing a minimal microscopic model**
 
 
First choose a minimal system with clear physical justification, rather than directly simulating a macroscopic object.
 
 
The following must be obtained:
 
 
- Microscopic Hamiltonian;
 
 
- particle-number and charge conservation;
 
 
- current operator;
 
 
- energy-momentum tensor;
 
 
- Coupling to the B(t) background.
 
 
Here, "minimal" means the fewest degrees of freedom while remaining physically closed, not arbitrarily dropping difficult physical processes for computational convenience.
 
 
**Acceptance criterion:** equations, dimensions, symmetries, conservation laws, and gauge consistency pass review.
 
 
## **Phase 2: Solving the static matter state**
 
 
For a fixed set of B values, solve for candidate equilibrium states.
 
 
Focus on:
 
 
- whether electrons still have definable bound states;
 
 
- whether the composition of nuclei and other particles changes;
 
 
- whether new low-dimensional bound states appear;
 
 
- whether stable or metastable solutions exist;
 
 
- whether the equation of state is physically meaningful.
 
 
**Acceptance criterion:** obtain at least one matter state that has passed stability checks, or explicitly demonstrate that no such solution exists in the current candidate model.
 
 
## **Phase 3: Investigating dynamic reachability**
 
 
Change B continuously from the initial state to the target state.
 
 
Study:
 
 
- whether the adiabatic condition is satisfied;
 
 
- non-adiabatic excitations;
 
 
- particle escape and trapping;
 
 
- structural instabilities;
 
 
- differences in final states at different compression rates.
 
 
**Acceptance criterion:** the static candidate state must not only exist but also be reachable along the specified DCC path; otherwise it must be explicitly marked as unreachable.
 
 
## **Phase 4: Computing the microscopic current response**
 
 
Begin only after Phase 2/3 has yielded a qualified matter state.
 
 
Compute:
 
 
\\[ \sigma\_{ij}(\omega) \\]
 
 
and output:
 
 
- \\(n\_{2D}\\);
 
 
- \\(g\\);
 
 
- \\(v_F\\) (if a valid definition exists);
 
 
- \\(\omega\_{\rm end}\\);
 
 
- \\(f\_{\rm intra}\\);
 
 
- microscopic scattering mechanisms;
 
 
- current matrix elements;
 
 
- the full complex response function.
 
 
**Acceptance criterion:** spectral weight is derived from the same microscopic model, KK self-consistency, Drude approximation has been independently validated.
 
 
## **Phase 5: Connecting to G6/DC2**
 
 
Only at the end, pass the verified
 
 
\\[ \sigma_s(\omega) \\]
 
 
to the existing optical chain.
 
 
The original S1–S15 and v1.3 specifications remain acceptance constraints and must not be relaxed to accommodate the new microscopic model.
 
 
If R1-B1 yields a real response that violates any constraint, one must determine whether the microscopic model is inapplicable, an approximation fails, or the scope of the specification itself needs re-examination; parameters must not be modified simply to make it pass.
 
 
# **VIII. The three most important technical risks, in my view**
 
 
| Risk        | Why it matters                      | Handling principle                    |
 
 
| --------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------- |
 
 
| wrong choice of microscopic degrees of freedom   | the entire model may rest on atomic, electronic, or band structures that no longer exist | first prove the applicability of the degrees of freedom              |
 
 
| stable state is unreachable    | an equilibrium solution at the final geometry does not imply that actual compression can reach it        | separate static and dynamic calculations               |
 
 
| incomplete spectral weight in the effective model | low-energy models may miss high-energy transitions, leading to an incorrect f-sum value.   | derive it from a complete microscopic theory or a model with explicit UV completeness. |
 
 
The third point in particular is directly relevant to your current optics research line.
 
 
If an effective two-dimensional Dirac model is used, one must explain how high-energy bands, other transitions, and the integrated-out degrees of freedom contribute to the total spectral weight. One cannot claim to have obtained the complete optical response of the real matter solely from the conductivity formula of a single low-energy cone.
